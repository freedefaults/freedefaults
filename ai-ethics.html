AI Ethics

One of the biggest ethical issues with AI and ML is that a system can work as designed and still cause harm, which the Week 3 slides explain through algorithmic bias, calibrated trust, and dual-use. In Lab 2, I saw our image classifier struggle when we showed it objects or poses outside its training examples. This showed me how limited a model can be when it encounters something unfamiliar, which becomes more concerning when its output affects someone’s opportunities or safety. In Lab 3, I discussed how employment gaps could act as a proxy for age or disability in the Workday hiring case, potentially putting qualified applicants at a disadvantage. These were allegations, not proven discrimination, but saying there is human oversight does not automatically address whether the scoring is fair. How is someone supposed to have an equal opportunity if the system screening them is already working against them? Calibrated trust was another concern. Both ChatGPT and Gemini recognized the breathing emergency in my tests, but a few good responses do not prove they will remain reliable throughout a longer conversation. People could trust that reassurance too much and delay seeking help. Voice cloning also demonstrates dual-use because a tool with legitimate purposes can be used to impersonate family members and steal money. I believe responsibility falls on both the user and the creator, with companies building in consent verification and human review of suspicious activity instead of leaving people to deal with the consequences themselves.
